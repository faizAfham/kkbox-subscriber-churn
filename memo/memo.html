Analysis memo · Mohammad Faiz Ahmad · November 2026 · Data: KKBox (WSDM Cup 2018), 760,163 paying monthly subscribers

KKBox loses most paying subscribers to cancellations, but the cheapest fix is the silent exits: test payment recovery first, then 149 on new sign-ups

Question. Where is KKBox losing paying monthly subscribers, and what should it fix first?

Answer. Of the 760,163 auto-renewing subscribers whose plan expired in March 2017, 2.97% left. 80% of those pressed cancel first; the other 20% left silently, with auto-renew still on. Silent exits are concentrated in three payment methods and, unlike cancellations, are more common among heavy listeners, which is what failed renewal charges look like.

Recommendation. (1) Test payment recovery (retries plus an "update your payment method" prompt) on methods 40, 30, 29: about TWD 9 M a year if it wins back a quarter of silent exits, readable in about six weeks. (2) Test 149 against 99 for new sign-ups: it pays unless it loses more than 25% of sign-ups. (3) Treat cancellations as a research problem, not a model: listening barely predicts them.

1. Context

KKBox is a Taiwanese music-streaming service that released real, anonymised subscriber data for the WSDM Cup 2018 churn challenge: 23 million payments from 2015 to March 2017, 6.8 million profiles, 392 million days of listening, and an official label saying which subscribers whose plan expired in March 2017 did not renew within 30 days. That label is the definition of churn throughout.

A subscription business has three levers on revenue from existing subscribers: keep the people who decide to leave, keep the people who leave without deciding, and charge more. This memo sizes each lever, ranks them, and designs the test for each of the top two.

2. Goals and non-goals

Goals. Split churn into its sources; find where each source is concentrated; size each in TWD; decide what to test first and design the tests.

Non-goals. A churn-prediction model (section 7 shows the cheap signals are weak, and a model doesn't tell a product team what to change); the optimal price (five prices are not a demand curve); manual-renewal subscribers, who churn 27% a month and are a different product decision (section 4).

3. Who is counted

From 970,960 labelled users, I keep those whose subscription expiring in March is a 30-day plan, on auto-renew, at a paid standard price (99, 100, 129, 149 or 180): 760,163 subscribers. A subscriber's plan, price and payment method come from their last valid payment on or before 28 February. I didn't use the March payment, because it is often the renewal itself, which would leak the outcome. For the price comparison I go further and keep only payment method 41, the one method that carries every tier from 99 to 149 (531,743 subscribers).

Ledger of 22,975,416 payments after removing 3,339 exact duplicates; seven automated data checks pass. 3,406 subscribers' expiring payment is itself a cancellation row; they are counted as cancellers.

4. Where churn comes from: four in five churners pressed cancel

A churner either cancelled (a cancellation on or after the expiring payment) or left silently (no cancellation, auto-renew still on, and no renewal within 30 days). For an auto-renewing subscriber, a silent exit means the renewal charge didn't go through: an expired card, a failed carrier bill, or a payment method that stopped working.

5. Silent exits: concentrated in three payment methods, and they hit engaged listeners

Silent churn averages 0.59% a month, but three methods run at two to four times that: method 40 (2.48%), method 30 (2.03%), method 29 (1.73%). Together they cover 92,479 subscribers and about half of all silent exits.

The evidence that these are failed charges rather than quiet decisions comes from listening. Subscribers who listened on 22–28 days in February cancel half as often as those who didn't listen at all (1.63% vs 3.29%), but they leave silently more often (0.74% vs 0.42%). People who use the product every day don't drift away; their payment fails. This is still an inference: KKBox's data has no "charge failed" field, so the first step is to ask the payments team for decline logs on these three methods.

A correction to an earlier draft. Method 39 churns 54% at 149, and I had read it as a billing failure. With cancellations counted from January rather than only March, almost all of those churners cancelled (53.9% via cancel, 0.05% silent), spread across 71 different days. It is a small group (2,116 subscribers) of people deliberately leaving or moving to another method, not a payments problem.

6. Price: 149 pays for existing subscribers, but the real unknown is sign-ups

The tiers are plans people chose, not prices they reacted to. 99.5% of subscribers whose previous plan was 99 are still on 99, and the same holds for every tier, so the data contains almost no price changes. What it can show is how people who chose 149 behave compared with people who chose 99. Within payment method 41 and sign-up channel 7, which carries every tier, 149 churns 3.24% a month against 1.65% for 99.

Under the decision rule committed to git before any churn rate was computed, the verdict is 149 pays: TWD 410 more per subscriber a year (95% CI 401–418), and churn would have to reach 9.86% before 99 earns more. It survives the obvious challenges. Tenure doesn't explain it (Mantel-Haenszel odds ratio 2.07 within tenure bands). It holds with 3 extra points of churn at 149 and on prices actually paid. The 908 subscribers whose price rose to 149 churn 5.3%, well under break-even.

So the price question moves to sign-ups. A higher price hurts most at the moment someone decides whether to subscribe, and this data can't see that moment. The useful number is the sign-up loss 149 can afford: 149 still earns more than 99 unless it loses more than 25% of the people who would have subscribed at 99 (1 − R99/R149, all payment methods). About 9,802 new paid subscriptions a month start at 99; moving them to 149 with no loss would be worth up to TWD 42 M a year. That is the ceiling, and only a test can tell how much of it is real.

7. Can listening warn us before someone cancels? Barely

The obvious retention play is to spot disengaged subscribers before renewal and win them back. Using only listening that happened before 28 February (392,106,543 log rows), churn falls from 3.71% for subscribers who didn't listen in February to 2.37% for near-daily listeners. That is a real gradient but a weak one. A flag for "listened 7 days or fewer, or listening stopped or halved" catches 64% of later cancellers but flags 52% of everyone, a lift of only 1.2×. An earlier draft reported a much steeper drop (3.30% to 0.72%), but that used March listening, which partly measures people who had already left.

A quarter of paying subscribers (26%) didn't listen at all in February and still mostly renewed. Waking them up with a "we miss you" message could just as easily remind them to cancel, so any re-engagement campaign needs a holdout group.

8. What each problem is worth, and what to do first

Cancellations are the biggest pool, but there is no cheap way to target them (section 7) and no test I can design from this data alone; the next step is research into why people cancel (an exit survey in the cancel flow), not a model. Payment recovery is smaller but cheap to build, low-risk to users, and fast to read, so it goes first. The price test is worth more if it works, but it touches every new subscriber and takes longer to read, so it goes second.

9. The two tests

Test 1: payment recovery on methods 40, 30, 29

  • Who: auto-renewing subscribers on these methods whose plan expires during the test, randomised 50/50 by user, stratified by method. About 92,479 become eligible each month.
  • Treatment: on a failed renewal charge, retry on days 1, 3 and 7, and send an in-app and email prompt with a one-tap link to update the payment method. Control: current behaviour.
  • Primary metric: share who renew within 30 days of expiry (1 − churn). Today 6.69% churn.
  • Guardrails: cancellation rate (a prompt can remind people to cancel; ship only if it rises by under 0.3 points), complaints and refund requests.
  • Size: winning back a quarter of silent exits moves churn from 6.69% to 6.11%; detecting that at 80% power and α = 0.05 needs 13,631 subscribers per arm, about 9 days of eligible subscribers, plus the 30-day renewal window: a readout in about six weeks.
  • Ship rule: renewal rate higher with a 95% interval above zero, and the cancellation guardrail holds.

Test 2: 149 vs 99 for new monthly sign-ups (payment method 41)

  • Who: new visitors reaching the plan page, randomised by account at the offer screen to see 99 or 149. Existing subscribers are untouched.
  • Primary metric: 12-month revenue per assigned visitor = sign-up rate × revenue per subscriber, counting everyone assigned, including those who don't subscribe.
  • Leading indicator, read first: sign-up rate. Stop early for 149 if it drops by more than 25%, because past that line 149 can't win. At an assumed 5% plan-page conversion, detecting a 25% drop needs about 2,147 visitors per arm.
  • Then: first-renewal churn, which needs 13,273 sign-ups per arm to detect a 0.5-point rise from 4.18%. At about 20,805 new 99 and 149 sign-ups a month, that is roughly 1.3 months of sign-ups plus one renewal cycle.
  • Guardrails: plan mix (do people switch to 129 or 180 instead?), refunds, and complaints from people who compare prices; randomise by account and device to limit that.
  • Ship rule: revenue per assigned visitor higher at 149 with a 95% interval above zero, and no guardrail worse by more than 1 point.

10. Risks, limits, and what would change my mind

  • "Silent" is inferred, not observed. If decline logs show these exits aren't failed charges, Test 1 loses its basis; it drops to a smaller experiment on whatever the logs do show.
  • One month stands in for a year. All annual figures assume every month looks like March 2017.
  • Observational price comparison. People chose their tier. Test 2 is the fix.
  • Music, Taiwan, 2017. The method transfers to a video service; the numbers don't.
  • Age and gender are valid for only 12% of subscribers, so I didn't use them.
  • What would change my mind: decline logs showing silent exits aren't failed payments; a sign-up drop above 25% at 149; or a cancel-flow survey showing cancellations driven by something fixable, which would put cancellations first.
